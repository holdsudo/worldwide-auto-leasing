Ending a car lease early: what your lease has to tell you

Early termination is where a lease can get expensive. The federal rule says what the paperwork has to disclose, and what rights you have if the car is valued low.

Published October 26, 2026

Why this matters before you sign, not after

Most people read the monthly payment and the amount due at signing. The sections that decide what a lease costs when something goes wrong are further down the page: what happens if you end it early, and what you owe if the car is worth less than the lease assumed. The federal consumer leasing rule, Regulation M (12 CFR 1013.4), requires those sections to be in the paperwork. Here is what it says and how to read it.

The early termination disclosure

A covered lease has to state the conditions under which either you or the lessor may end the lease before the scheduled date. It also has to state the amount of any early termination penalty or other charge, or the method for working it out, and the rule says the charge must be reasonable.

For motor vehicle leases, the rule also requires a notice substantially like this: you may have to pay a substantial charge if you end the lease early, the charge may be up to several thousand dollars, it depends on when you terminate, and the earlier you end it, the greater the charge is likely to be. If your lease does not contain that warning, ask why.

  • Look for the method, not just a sentence. "Remaining payments plus fees, less what the car sells for" is a method. "Charges may apply" is not.
  • Ask for a written early-termination figure for a specific date. The number changes every month.
  • Ask how the car's value will be set. The rule describes realized value as the price the lessor receives at disposition, the highest offer for disposition, or the fair market value at the end of the lease term.

Your right to an appraisal

If what you owe at early termination or at the end of the term depends on the realized value of the vehicle, the lease must say you may obtain, at your own expense, a professional appraisal of the value that could be realized at sale, by an independent third party that you and the lessor agree on. Under the rule, that appraisal is final and binding on both of you.

That is a real lever. If the lessor's number looks low, the appraisal gives you a neutral figure. It costs money, so it makes sense when the gap between the lessor's value and a fair one is large.

The three-payment presumption

For a lease where you are liable at the end of the term for the difference between the residual value and the realized value, Regulation M requires a statement about a rebuttable presumption. The presumption is that the residual value is unreasonable and not in good faith to the extent it exceeds the realized value by more than three times the base monthly payment. The lessor cannot collect that excess unless it brings a successful court action and pays your reasonable attorney's fees, or unless the gap is due to unreasonable or excessive wear or use of the vehicle.

The point is the benchmark: a gap of more than three payments is the point where the rule presumes the lessor's residual was set unreasonably. The rule also says you and the lessor may make any mutually agreeable final adjustment after the lease ends.

Which leases this affects

Most consumer car leases are closed-end, and the Federal Reserve's leasing guide notes that on a closed-end lease you are not responsible for a shortfall between the depreciation you paid and the car's actual depreciation, as long as the payments are made. The shortfall provisions above apply where the lease makes you liable for the difference, which is typical of open-end leases. Early termination charges are a separate question and apply to closed-end leases too. Read which kind you signed.

If you are in the military

Active-duty servicemembers with qualifying orders have a separate right under federal law to end a car lease without an early termination charge. See our guide to military orders and your car lease.

Before you sign: a short list

  1. Find the early termination section and read the method, not only the warning.
  2. Ask whether the lease is closed-end or open-end, and whether you are liable for a residual shortfall.
  3. If you are liable for a shortfall, find the appraisal-right and three-payment statements.
  4. Ask for an early-termination figure at 12, 24 and 30 months, so you see how it falls over time.
  5. Keep a copy of everything you sign.

Common questions

Does my lease have to warn me about early termination charges?
For motor vehicle leases covered by Regulation M, yes. The rule requires a notice that you may have to pay a substantial charge, possibly up to several thousand dollars, and that the earlier you end the lease the greater the charge is likely to be.
Can I get an independent appraisal of my leased car?
If your liability at early termination or at the end of the term depends on the car's realized value, the lease must state that you may obtain a professional appraisal by an independent third party agreed to by both sides, at your expense. The rule says the appraisal is final and binding.
What is the three-payment rule?
It is a rebuttable presumption in Regulation M that, where you are liable for the difference, a residual value is unreasonable and not in good faith to the extent it exceeds the realized value by more than three times the base monthly payment. Exceptions apply, including excessive wear or use.
